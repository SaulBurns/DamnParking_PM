Aggregate findings

Across student/staff interviews, informal lot observation near peak class changes, and a teardown of payment-first vs. guidance-first parking products, three patterns repeated:

  1. Availability beats payment UX. Drivers already pay (permits, meters, apps). The recurring curse word is circling — not checkout friction. Products that only sell transactions leave the “is there a spot?” question unanswered.
  2. Trust requires live truth, not static maps. Static “lot is usually full” guidance decays under events, construction, and semester rhythm. Users punish apps that send them to a full row. Facilities staff separately want the same truth for enforcement staffing and lot planning.
  3. Hardware-heavy stall sensors don’t match campus economics. In-ground sensors and per-stall IoT look polished in vendor decks and collapse under retrofit cost, asphalt work, and maintenance across dozens of lots. Camera reuse keeps showing up as the only path that facilities contacts treat as remotely fundable.

Comparable landscape we studied (not exhaustive): ParkMobile / payment aggregators (transaction layer, weak occupancy); SpotHero / reservation marketplaces (urban inventory, not campus camera ops); Park Assist and similar camera/guidance vendors (closest technical cousins — enterprise sales cycles, heavy install narratives); generic “smart city” dashboards (pretty occupancy tiles, unclear bay-level accuracy claims).

Segment signal: campus drivers want “which lot / which aisle has space before I commit”; facilities / parking services want aggregate fill curves and exception alerts; event ops want temporary overlays. We narrowed primary users to daily campus drivers + parking services as the dual customer for an MVP.

Top pick — deep dive

Camera-based bay occupancy on existing lot CCTV, published as live counts + a simple map.

This rose to the top because it intersects the three patterns: it answers availability (not just payment), it can be live, and it avoids per-stall hardware. Evidence that backed the pick:

  • Problem frequency: Peak-period circling is observable and emotionally salient — interviewees described 5–15 minute hunts as normal on bad days, which is enough pain to change arrival behavior if a trustworthy signal exists.
  • Asset leverage: Many lots already have cameras for security. A software layer that consumes feeds (with policy approval) is a different budget conversation than ripping asphalt for sensors.
  • MVP sharpness: Success can be defined narrowly — “open vs occupied per bay in Lot X during daylight, with a confidence flag” — without boiling the ocean into autonomous valet.
  • Dual value: The same occupancy stream serves drivers (where to go) and ops (how full we are), which strengthens the sponsor story.
Why not only a mobile “report a spot” social layer? Crowdsourced spot reports die from cold-start and vandalism (false opens). Vision from fixed cameras is the source of truth; human reports can be a later supplement, not the spine.

What didn’t work / what we pivoted from

Abandoned: reservation marketplace for campus stalls. Attractive on paper (monetize scarcity). Broke down under permit politics, fairness optics, and the fact that most campus stalls aren’t a SpotHero-style inventory the university wants to auction to students.
Abandoned: greenfield IoT stall sensors as the primary sensing plan. Accuracy narratives are strong; CapEx and maintenance across campus lots are not. We keep sensors as a possible future augmentation for covered or high-value bays, not the MVP foundation.
Abandoned: “build our own cameras first.” Early excitement about custom hardware competed with the real bottleneck — software, data policy, and proving occupancy quality. Pivot: assume existing feeds + mount permissions; hardware only if a lot is blind.

Assumption that changed: we stopped treating Damn Parking as a consumer payment app with a map skin, and started treating it as an occupancy intelligence service whose first customer is campus parking services — with drivers as the visible beneficiaries.
